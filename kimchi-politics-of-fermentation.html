Kimchi and the
Politics of Fermentation

A diplomatic incident over a pickle standard. A thousand years of white kimchi before the red kind existed. A national dish that has been to space. This is why a fermented cabbage carries the weight it does.

KoreaFermentation · Identity · History9 min read

In November 2020, the International Organization for Standardization published document ISO 24220:2020 — a technical standard for pao cai, the salted fermented vegetables of China's Sichuan province. On its face, it was one of the least dramatic documents ever issued by a standards body. Within days it had produced a formal statement from South Korea's Ministry of Agriculture, furious editorials in Seoul newspapers, and a public argument between two governments. The subject of all this fury was a dish of fermented cabbage.

What happened was a collision of language and pride. China's Global Times reported the new standard as "an international standard for the kimchi industry led by China." In Korea, the claim landed as an attempted annexation. It was also, in a narrow sense, a translation problem: in Chinese, pao cai works as a general term for pickled vegetables, and Korean kimchi sold in China is routinely labeled with the same words. The ISO document itself stated plainly, in its own text, that it did not apply to kimchi. South Korea's agriculture ministry made exactly that point, formally — it was inappropriate, the ministry held, to report the pao cai standard without distinguishing it from kimchi. The Global Times eventually attributed its phrasing to a translation error. None of it lowered the temperature. Because the argument was never really about a standards document.

The argument was never really about the vegetables. It was about whose name goes on them — and in food, a name is a deed of ownership.

Kimchi almost never appears alone. It arrives as one of the banchan — the small dishes that surround a Korean meal and turn a table into a landscape. We explored that architecture in our story on japchae and the culture of banchan.

The Red Kimchi Is the New Kimchi

Start with the surprise: for most of its history, kimchi was not red, not spicy, and not made from the cabbage the world now pictures. Koreans have been fermenting vegetables in brine for well over a thousand years. The Samguk Sagi, the twelfth-century history of Korea's Three Kingdoms, mentions jars used for fermenting vegetables. In 1241, the scholar Yi Gyu-bo wrote a poem praising radish salted down to last through the winter — a jar, salt, and time were the whole technology. The dish's older name, chimchae, means something like "submerged vegetables." It was a survival system before it was a cuisine: a way to keep something alive and edible through winters that killed everything green.

The chili pepper — an American plant, carried around the world by European ships — reached Korea around the turn of the seventeenth century; the first clear Korean record of it, from 1614, does not mention eating it. Chili appears in a kimchi recipe for the first time in a household manual published in 1766, and only in the nineteenth century did the red style become the standard. The tight-headed napa cabbage that defines modern baechu-kimchi is younger still — a fixture of roughly the last hundred years. The white kimchis never disappeared: baek-kimchi, mild and elegant, and dongchimi, radish floating in its own clear brine, are the old dish still sitting on the table. The red kimchi the world treats as timeless is, in food-history terms, a recent invention — which may be exactly why it is defended so fiercely. It is not an heirloom. It is an achievement.

It helps to understand that kimchi is a method, not a recipe. The method — salt, season, ferment — is applied to whatever the season hands over: cabbage in the cold months, cucumber and young radish in summer, leafy greens in between. Households guard their own proportions, and Korean has a word for the variable no recipe can capture: son-mat, "hand taste," the individual signature a particular cook leaves on a dish. Two families can follow the same written recipe for the same cabbage and produce two different kimchis, and both will insist, correctly, that theirs is the real one. A dish with that much built-in variation was always going to resist standardization. Remember that when the standards bodies arrive, later in this story.

An Entire Country, Fermenting

Every late autumn, Korea performs gimjang: the communal making of enough kimchi to last the winter. Families, neighbors, and historically whole villages salt cabbages by the hundred, work in the seasoning — gochugaru, garlic, ginger, scallion, and jeotgal, salted fermented seafood — and pack the finished kimchi into onggi, the great earthenware crocks that were traditionally buried in the ground to hold a steady temperature. In 2013, UNESCO inscribed gimjang on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, describing a practice that reaffirms Korean identity and is handed down from one generation to the next. Two years later, North Korea's tradition of kimchi-making was inscribed as well — the divided peninsula holding the same heritage in two separate entries.

The sharing is structural, not decorative. Gimjang production was always scaled beyond a single household's needs, and the surplus moved — to neighbors, to relatives, to families whose own crocks had failed or whose winter looked thin. That logic survives in modern form: community gimjang events, where volunteers make kimchi in enormous batches for low-income households, are a fixture of the Korean late-autumn calendar. UNESCO's listing was careful on this point — it inscribed not a recipe but a practice of making and sharing, a food system whose second half is distribution. The crock is a pantry. It is also a welfare system.

The modern country built infrastructure around the dish. Korean appliance makers sell dedicated kimchi refrigerators, engineered to hold fermentation along a precise curve. And in April 2008, when Yi So-yeon became the first Korean in space, flying to the International Space Station aboard a Russian Soyuz, Korean food scientists had spent years developing ten Korean space foods for the mission. Two of them were kimchi — one freeze-dried, one canned, both irradiated to destroy the very bacteria that make kimchi kimchi. Yi hosted a Korean food night for the station's crew. A dish invented to survive the Korean winter had been re-engineered to survive orbit.

Essential Knowledge · Korea

Kimchi is not one dish. Well over a hundred varieties are documented — cabbage, radish, cucumber, leafy greens — unified by method rather than ingredient: vegetables salted, seasoned, and fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which produce the acidity, preserve the food, and keep it slowly changing in the jar. The seasoning paste for the classic cabbage version combines gochugaru (chili flakes), garlic, ginger, and jeotgal (salted fermented seafood). In Korea, kimchi is not a side dish so much as a condition of the table — it is served at effectively every meal.

Why It Matters Beyond Korea

The 2020 row had a prequel. In the 1990s, the fight was with Japan, whose producers sold a lightly pickled version under the name kimuchi; Korea insisted the name belonged to the fermented dish. In 2001, the Codex Alimentarius — the international food standards body — adopted a standard for kimchi that defined it as a fermented product, settling the name, and its Korean spelling, in Korea's favor. Then, in 2021, in the aftermath of the pao cai incident, South Korea's culture ministry adopted a new official Chinese rendering of the word — xin qi — created specifically so that kimchi would no longer share a name with pao cai in Chinese. Read the three episodes together and a pattern emerges: every fight was about a definition, and every definition was about credit. The fermentation is done by bacteria. The ownership is assigned by paperwork.

The xin qi solution, it should be said, pleased almost nobody. Reporting at the time found Chinese readers objecting to being instructed on how to translate, and Korean readers objecting that kimchi should need a Chinese name at all — as if the word itself had become territory that could be ceded. Both reactions rather prove the underlying point. Nobody fights this hard over a food they consider ordinary, and nobody issues naming guidelines for one either. The paperwork escalates precisely in proportion to the meaning.

That is the real subject of the politics of fermentation. Kimchi spent a millennium as anonymous infrastructure — salted vegetables in a buried crock, too ordinary to argue about. It became worth fighting over when it became a symbol: of a country that industrialized within a generation without surrendering its table, that put its pickle into orbit, that treats a crock of cabbage as a carrier of identity. Nations do not issue ministerial statements about side dishes. They issue them about flags. Buy a jar of the real thing — refrigerated, still fermenting, alive in the jar. Taste it this week, and taste it again in three weeks. It will not be the same dish. That is the education.
