The Cheese Store of Beverly Hills: An Instagram Page Worth Getting Lost In

I have never been there—but its food-filled reels make a compelling case for slowing down, watching and learning.

Words by Don't Be Hangry  ·  Deeper Dive

I have never been to The Cheese Store of Beverly Hills. That needs to be said upfront. This is not a restaurant review disguised as a firsthand experience. I cannot tell you how the store smells when the door opens, how crowded the counter gets at lunchtime or which cheese a member of the staff would place in front of me after hearing what I normally enjoy.

What I can tell you is that I found its Instagram page—and stopped scrolling.

For anyone interested in cheese, Italian food, sandwiches or the quiet craftsmanship behind exceptional ingredients, The Cheese Store of Beverly Hills has created an unusually satisfying place to spend some time online.

The Short Version

  • The Cheese Store of Beverly Hills has operated since 1967.
  • The store says it carries more than 600 cheeses from around the world.
  • Its selection extends to charcuterie, caviar, truffles, wine, pasta, sauces and other specialty foods.
  • Its short videos are entertaining, but they also teach viewers how cheese is made, aged, cut and appreciated.
  • I have not visited the store; this article is about what makes its online storytelling worth following.

More than an expensive cheese counter

The Cheese Store of Beverly Hills traces its history to 1967, when Colonel Sigmund Roth founded a shop centered on exotic cheeses and specialty foods. Norbert Wabnig took ownership in 1978 and spent the following decades shaping it into a Los Angeles institution.

In 2022, Wabnig sold the business to Dominick DiBartolomeo, who had already worked at the store for approximately 20 years. The following year, the business moved from its longtime Beverly Drive address to a larger location on South Santa Monica Boulevard. The expanded store created more room for imported foods and added a café serving sandwiches, salads and prepared dishes.

The store now says it carries more than 600 cheeses sourced from around the world, along with charcuterie, caviar, seasonal truffles, wine, spirits, pasta, sauces, sweets and other specialty goods. It is part neighborhood institution, part international food library and part ongoing lesson in how much variation can exist inside something as seemingly simple as cheese.

The reels are like going to cheese school

Most food accounts simply show finished dishes. They deliver a quick glamour shot, trigger a few seconds of hunger and then disappear into the feed.

The Cheese Store's reels do something more interesting. Watching them is like going to cheese school—without the tuition, textbooks or final exam.

A wheel is opened. A rind is explained. A cheesemonger cuts into something most viewers have probably never encountered. You see the texture of the interior, the crystallized edge of an aged cheese or the blue mold running through a wedge. Just as importantly, you begin to understand why those details matter.

The reels introduce unfamiliar cheeses in a way that feels enthusiastic rather than intimidating. You gradually see that cheese is not one simple category. It is shaped by milk, geography, aging, bacteria, mold, salt, temperature and human judgment. A firm Alpine cheese and a soft triple-cream may share the same refrigerated case, but they represent completely different traditions and eating experiences.

The store's online cheese finder reflects that same depth, organizing selections by style, texture, milk and region—from Alpine and Gruyère varieties to Brie, Gouda, Parmigiano Reggiano, blue cheese, goat cheese, Manchego, Pecorino and washed-rind cheeses.

Why the page works: You may arrive for the food photography, but you stay because someone is teaching you something.

Then there are the sandwiches

Cheese may be the institution's foundation, but it is not the entire story. The café uses imported cheeses, charcuterie and olive oil sold inside the store, along with bread baked daily. The current location offers dine-in service, pickup and local delivery.

This is where the account becomes dangerous if you happen to be hungry. The sandwiches look substantial without appearing carelessly overloaded. They are built around the ingredients rather than engineered merely for height. Melted cheese, sliced meat, vegetables, sauces and good bread are allowed to look like what they are.

There is confidence in that presentation. When the ingredients are exceptional, there is no need to bury them beneath a stunt.

A store that makes expertise approachable

Specialty-food stores can occasionally feel intimidating. Nobody wants to stand at a counter pretending to understand the difference between two unfamiliar French cheeses while an expert waits for an answer.

The best cheesemongers remove that pressure. They translate expertise into ordinary questions: What flavors do you already enjoy? Do you want something mild or assertive? Creamy or firm? Familiar or strange? Are you building a board, cooking dinner or simply looking for something good to eat with a piece of bread?

The Cheese Store's website follows that same approach with a short taste-profile feature designed to suggest a familiar starting point, a logical next step and one more adventurous selection. Good food knowledge should invite people inside, not make them feel unqualified to enter.

Why it is worth following

I cannot personally recommend that you travel across Los Angeles and buy a particular sandwich. I have not eaten one. I cannot name the best cheese in the case or tell you which item justifies its price.

But I can recommend looking at the page.

The appeal is not limited to people who can visit Beverly Hills. The account offers a window into a food culture built on selection, craft and curiosity. It reminds you that an ingredient can carry geography and history with it—and that someone behind a counter may have spent years learning how to recognize the best version.

Sometimes social media makes food look artificial, excessive or disposable. This page does the opposite. It makes you want to slow down, ask what you are looking at and learn how it was made.

And then, inevitably, it makes you want a sandwich.
