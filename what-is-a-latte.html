What Is a Latte? Espresso, Milk and the Drink Behind the Name

It looks simple—espresso and milk—but the latte became a worldwide café standard precisely because it leaves room for interpretation.

Words by Don't Be Hangry  ·  Deeper Dive

In most contemporary specialty coffee shops, a latte is an espresso-based drink made with a substantial amount of steamed milk and a relatively thin layer of foam. That broad definition is more reliable than any universal ratio. Cup sizes, espresso doses and milk texture vary from one café and country to another.

The Short Version

  • A modern café latte usually combines espresso with steamed milk.
  • Traditional Italian caffè latte may instead use coffee brewed in a moka pot or by another method.
  • It is generally milkier and less foam-forward than a cappuccino.
  • There is no single worldwide recipe or mandatory coffee-to-milk ratio.
  • The Italian words caffè latte mean “coffee and milk.”

The name is literal

In Italian, latte means milk. The fuller phrase caffè latte means coffee and milk. Ordering only “latte” in Italy means asking for milk, not necessarily the espresso drink expected in an English-speaking café.

Traditional Italian caffè latte is commonly a breakfast drink made at home with hot milk and coffee, which may come from a moka pot rather than an espresso machine. The large espresso-and-steamed-milk version familiar in contemporary coffee shops developed its own identity through modern café culture. Claims that one person or one café simply “invented” the latte flatten a much older coffee-and-milk tradition into a cleaner story than the evidence supports.

What is actually in the cup?

The basic construction is espresso, steamed milk and a light surface of microfoam. The espresso provides concentration and bitterness; the milk adds sweetness, body and volume. Properly textured milk is glossy and uniform, with tiny bubbles rather than a dry cap of large foam.

A latte may use one or more espresso shots depending on cup size and house recipe. That is why two drinks sold under the same name can taste very different. A larger milk volume without an additional shot produces a gentler coffee flavor; an extra shot restores intensity.

Latte, cappuccino or flat white?

The boundaries are practical, not universal laws. A cappuccino is generally smaller and presents a more noticeable balance of espresso, steamed milk and foam. A latte is commonly larger, with more liquid milk and a thinner foam layer. A flat white is usually smaller than a latte and emphasizes espresso beneath finely textured milk, often with less visible foam.

The honest rule: names describe a family resemblance. The exact cup size, shot count and milk texture depend on the café, so ask if the distinction matters to you.

Why milk texture matters

Steaming does two jobs: it warms the milk and incorporates air. The goal for a latte is microfoam—a smooth mixture of liquid milk and very small bubbles. When the pitcher is swirled, well-textured milk should look glossy and pour as one integrated fluid.

Dairy milk works predictably because its proteins help stabilize foam and its lactose contributes sweetness as the drink warms. Plant-based alternatives behave differently by formulation. “Barista” versions are usually engineered to steam more consistently, but results still vary by brand and technique.

Latte art is technique, not a separate drink

Hearts, rosettas and tulips appear when a barista controls the height and speed of the pour. Milk poured from higher above sinks beneath the crema; milk poured close to the surface leaves a white pattern. The design can signal good milk texture and pouring control, but a beautiful pattern does not guarantee balanced espresso or correct temperature.

Why the latte travels so well

The latte is adaptable. It can be served hot or iced, made in different sizes and flavored without losing its basic identity. That flexibility helped it become a default café order, but it also explains why rigid online diagrams often mislead. The drink is defined by its structure—espresso softened by plenty of milk—more than by one mathematically fixed recipe.
