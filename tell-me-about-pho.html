Tell Me About Pho: Vietnam's Bowl of Broth, Noodles and History

Pho looks simple. A proper bowl carries a century of migration, regional taste and patient broth-making.

Words by Don't Be Hangry  ·  Deeper Dive

Pho is not merely noodle soup with a handful of herbs tossed on top. It is a careful arrangement of broth, flat rice noodles, meat and aromatics in which the broth does most of the talking. Everything else either supports that broth or gets in its way.

The Short Version

  • Pho is a Vietnamese soup built around broth and flat rice noodles called bánh phở.
  • Phở bò uses beef; phở gà uses chicken.
  • The modern dish emerged in northern Vietnam around the turn of the 20th century, but its exact origin is debated.
  • Northern bowls are generally restrained; southern bowls commonly arrive sweeter and with a larger plate of herbs and garnishes.
  • Taste the broth before reaching for lime, chile, hoisin or sriracha.

First, the name

The Vietnamese word is written phở. The vowel and tone matter in Vietnamese, and the closest practical English approximation is closer to “fuh” than “foe.” The word also refers to the flat rice noodles at the center of the dish.

A young dish with a complicated family tree

Compared with many foods treated as ancient tradition, pho is relatively young. Its modern form is generally traced to northern Vietnam in the late 19th or early 20th century. Nam Định is often associated with its geographical beginnings, while Hanoi became its cultural and commercial home.

The tidy origin stories are tempting. One credits French colonial demand for beef and the resulting availability of bones. Another points to Chinese noodle traditions and mobile vendors. Even the suggestion that the name descends from the French stew pot-au-feu is debated. The safest conclusion is also the most interesting: pho took shape in northern Vietnam where Vietnamese technique met new ingredients, markets and outside influences.

The broth is the dish

Beef pho begins with bones and meat cooked long enough to produce body, then seasoned so the finished stock remains aromatic rather than muddy. Charred onion and ginger supply sweetness and depth. Star anise, cinnamon, clove and related warm spices may be used, but none should turn the bowl into liquid potpourri. Fish sauce brings salt and savoriness. A skilled cook aims for a broth that tastes clear, layered and complete before anything is added at the table.

Chicken pho is not a lesser substitute. Phở gà has its own cleaner broth, typically supported by ginger, onion and chicken rather than the dark richness of beef bones. In either version, fresh rice noodles are briefly heated, placed in the bowl and covered with broth hot enough to warm the noodles and finish thin slices of raw beef when that cut is ordered.

One name, more than one regional personality

Hanoi-style pho is usually the more austere bowl. The broth tends to be clear and savory, the garnish list short, and the cook's seasoning left largely intact. Scallion, cilantro, chile and a squeeze of citrus may be enough.

Southern pho, especially the style associated with Ho Chi Minh City, often arrives with a fuller supporting cast: bean sprouts, Thai basil, culantro, lime and sliced chile, with hoisin and chile sauce available at the table. The broth may taste sweeter and the menu may offer more cuts of beef. These are broad regional tendencies, not rules followed by every shop.

What the garnish plate means: It is an invitation to adjust the bowl, not an order to dump everything in at once.

How to approach the bowl

Start with the broth. Take one spoonful before adding anything. That first taste tells you what the cook built and what, if anything, the bowl needs. Add herbs in small amounts so their aroma stays distinct. A squeeze of lime can sharpen a rich broth; sliced chile brings fresh heat.

If hoisin and chile sauce are provided, consider putting them in a small side dish for dipping meat. Pouring both heavily into the soup can erase the balance that took hours to create. There is no need to perform authenticity at the table, but there is a good reason to give the broth a chance before rebuilding it.

Ordering without guessing

Phở tái commonly means pho with thin rare beef that cooks in the hot broth. Chín indicates cooked or well-done beef. Depending on the restaurant, brisket, flank, tendon, tripe, meatballs and combinations may be available. Phở gà is chicken pho. Restaurant terminology and combinations vary, so a menu description is more useful than pretending every shop uses an identical code.

Why pho matters

Pho moved south within Vietnam, changed with regional tastes, and traveled abroad with Vietnamese communities after the Vietnam War. That journey helped make it one of the world's most recognizable Vietnamese foods. Its global familiarity can hide how precise it is: a bowl flexible enough to evolve, but structured enough that broth, noodles and balance still determine whether it succeeds.
