Feta and the
Protected Designation War

Registered in 1996. Annulled in 1999. Registered again in 2002, upheld in 2005, and fought over one more time in 2022. This is how a block of cheese in brine became Europe's longest-running argument about whether a name can belong to a place.

GreeceLaw · Identity · Preservation9 min read

On 25 October 2005, the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Justice delivered its judgment in joined cases C-465/02 and C-466/02. Germany and Denmark had asked the court to annul the regulation that protected the name “feta.” The court dismissed both actions. With that ruling — dry, procedural, and entirely without cheese — a dispute that had already consumed nearly a decade was, for the moment, settled: in the European Union, feta was Greek, as a matter of law. The governments on the losing side were not cranks or counterfeiters. They represented countries where dairies had made and legally sold a white brined cheese under that same name for decades. That is what made the case hard, and what still makes it interesting. Both sides were describing something real.

Start with what was never in dispute. White cheese made from sheep's and goat's milk and stored in brine has been produced around the eastern Mediterranean for a very long time. Popular accounts reach back to the cheese-making in Homer's Odyssey — the Cyclops curdling milk in his cave — but that scene is a literary echo, not a birth certificate; no one can date feta's invention. What can be said plainly is that in Greece, this cheese was not a specialty product. It was the everyday cheese of a sheep-and-goat landscape — rocky ground, hardy animals, milk that needed salt and brine to survive a warm climate — and it was eaten in quantities that made it, in effect, a national staple.

The Cheese Before the Case

The name itself contains the first complication. Most etymological accounts trace “feta” to the Italian fetta, meaning a slice — a reference, probably, to the cheese being sliced to pack it into barrels. The word became widespread as the cheese's name only in the nineteenth century. So the cheese is ancient in type, Greek in its developed form, and named, comparatively recently, with a borrowed Italian word for how it was cut. None of that weakened Greece's claim. It clarified what the claim actually was: not that Greeks invented brined white cheese — similar cheeses are traditional across the Balkans and around the Black Sea — but that the specific cheese called feta, made in a specific way from specific milk in a specific place, had a meaning that a generic white cheese did not.

Greece had been tightening that definition at home long before Brussels got involved. Greek rules for feta production were laid out in the 1930s and later expanded to protect the place of production, with the geographical rules enacted into Greek law in 1988, according to the European Commission's account of the case. Then, in 1992, the European Union created its Protected Designation of Origin system — the PDO some food names are not descriptions of a style but titles of origin, and using them for a product from somewhere else is a kind of falsehood, even if the recipe is copied faithfully. Champagne is the famous example. Feta would become the test of how far that premise could stretch — to a humble, everyday cheese, with no vineyard, no vintage, and no luxury price attached.

The specification Greece defended is precise, and its precision is the argument. Under the PDO, feta must be made in defined areas of mainland Greece — Macedonia, Thrace, Epirus, Thessaly, Central Greece, and the Peloponnese — and in the prefecture of Lesbos. The milk must be sheep's milk, or sheep's milk with no more than 30 percent goat's milk, from animals reared in that area and fed on its local flora. Cow's milk is not permitted. The curd is drained, dry-salted, and ripened in brine for at least two months, without colourants or preservatives. The theory inside that list: flavour lives not in the recipe, which anyone can copy, but in the chain — breed, pasture, milk, place — that no copy can reproduce.

A PDO does not protect a recipe. It protects the claim that a recipe is the least important part — that the place made the food, and the food cannot be unmade from the place.

A Name That Would Not Stay Registered

Greece applied to register feta as a PDO in 1994, and the name was first registered in 1996. Denmark, Germany, and France challenged that registration, and on 16 March 1999 the Court of Justice annulled it. The court held that the European Commission, in registering the name, had failed to take account of all the relevant factors for deciding whether “feta” had become a generic name — in particular, the situation in the member states where cheese had been lawfully produced and sold as feta for a long time. The registration fell on procedure, not on the merits. The question — generic word, or Greek name? — was sent back to be answered properly.

The other side's case deserves its full weight, because it was not weak. Danish, German, and French producers had made white cheese, much of it from cow's milk, and marketed it as feta lawfully and openly for decades. In Denmark especially, it had become a significant export industry. For many consumers outside Greece, “feta” simply meant the category: white, crumbly, salty, kept in brine — a style, in the way that shoppers treat many food names as styles. If a word has become the common name of a thing, EU law itself says it cannot be registered as a designation of origin. The challengers were not trying to steal a Greek word. They were arguing the word no longer belonged to anyone.

The Commission did the work again and on 14 October 2002, by Regulation No 1829/2002, feta was entered again in the register of protected designations of origin. Germany and Denmark sued again. This time the court reached the merits and dismissed the actions, accepting that “feta” had not become generic — the name still evoked Greece, and the concentration and long continuity of production and consumption there mattered. Since 2002, then — confirmed in 2005 — only cheese meeting the Greek specification can be sold as feta inside the European Union. Danish white cheese did not disappear. It reappeared under other names, of the “Greek-style salad cheese” kind: the same dairy case, a different deed to the word.

We have told a version of this story before, in a different key: in the Jollof Wars, the argument is about credit between neighbouring countries who share a dish. The feta case is the colder, legal form of the same question — what happens when the argument about credit is moved out of kitchens and newspapers and into a register, a regulation, and a court.

Essential Knowledge · Greece

PDO feta, in the European Union since 2002: a white brined cheese made only in defined areas of mainland Greece and the prefecture of Lesbos, from sheep's milk or a blend of at least 70 percent sheep's milk and up to 30 percent goat's milk, from animals reared in the area. No cow's milk. The curd is drained and dry-salted, then ripened in brine for at least two months, with no colourants or preservatives. Outside the EU the position varies: the name is protected in a number of other countries, while in the United States “feta” remains in generic use, and most American feta is made from cow's milk.

The Export Loophole That Wasn't

Most legal stories would end in 2005. Feta's had one more round. Danish producers had stopped using “feta” inside the EU, as the law required. But they continued to use it on cheese made in Denmark and exported to countries outside the EU, where the name was not protected. The European Commission, supported by Greece and Cyprus, disagreed, and brought Denmark before the court in case C-159/20.

On 14 July 2022, the court ruled for the Commission. It held that using a registered PDO name on a product produced in the EU that does not comply with the specification infringes the protection the regulation grants — including where the product is intended only for export to third countries — and that Denmark, by failing to prevent or stop that use by its dairy producers, had failed to fulfil its obligations under Article 13 of Regulation No 1151/2012.

Why It Matters Beyond Greece

It would be easy to file the feta war as Brussels bureaucracy at its most theatrical — twenty years of litigation, in the bench's shorthand, over a salad cheese. That reading misses what the case actually decided. If the PDO system could protect a name as ordinary, as widely imitated, and as weakly geographical as “feta” — a borrowed word for “slice,” attached to a cheese type made all around a sea — then the system meant what it said: that in Europe, a food name can be property of a place in the same way a trademark is property of a company,.

The honest counterargument did not die in 2005. Something was genuinely lost when a word millions of shoppers used as a category became, in one large market, one country's legal possession — Danish dairies that had built businesses in good faith had them redefined — and outside Europe the map is still a patchwork, the name protected in some countries and generic in others, the United States among them. A block labelled feta in an American supermarket and a block labelled feta in Athens are, legally, two different kinds of statement. The meaning of the name now depends on which side of a border you are standing on when you read the label.

That, in the end, is why the war lasted as long as it did, and why it is still the case lawyers reach for — the Advocate General in the 2022 case opened, in effect, by noting that it was feta again, at least the fourth instalment of the saga. Every side in every round believed it was defending something ordinary people are entitled to: Greeks, that a name their food culture built should not be diluted into a style; Danish and German producers, that a common word should stay common; the Commission and the court, that a promise printed on a label should be enforceable. Crumble a piece of the real thing — sheep's milk, brined, sharp enough to argue back — over a salad this week. You are tasting the specification. The twenty years of litigation are in there too, whether you can taste them or not.
