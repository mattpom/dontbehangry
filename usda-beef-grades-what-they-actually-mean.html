Misconception

The USDA Shield On Your Steak Isn't a Safety Inspection

It's a paid, voluntary opinion about fat — one that a meatpacker has to specifically request and pay for, and that didn't exist in any form until a one-year government trial run in 1927. Here's what Prime, Choice, and Select actually measure, and why the bar for each one has quietly moved at least three times since.

Pick up a package of beef at almost any grocery store and you'll likely see two different USDA marks on it, though most shoppers only register one. There's the round "USDA Inspected and Passed" stamp, which every animal slaughtered in a federally regulated plant is legally required to carry. And then there's the shield — "USDA Prime," "USDA Choice," "USDA Select" — which looks like it belongs to the same category of government oversight. It doesn't.

Inspection Is Mandatory. Grading Is a Purchase.

Federal meat inspection exists to confirm an animal was healthy, slaughtered under sanitary conditions, and safe to eat. It's free to the packer, it's required by law, and every piece of beef sold in the United States has to clear it. That's the round stamp.

Quality grading — the shield, and the letter grade inside it — is a completely separate program, and a voluntary one. A USDA-employed grader assesses the carcass for expected eating quality and stamps a grade on it, but only if the packing plant requests the service and pays for it. Nothing requires a producer to have their beef graded at all. A meaningful amount of beef sold in the U.S. is never graded — sold instead under a store's private label or a producer's own brand standard, with no USDA letter attached, and no implication that it's lower quality for lacking one.

Where the Grades Came From

The system traces back to a 1916 law that established the National Livestock Market News Service, created to give producers and buyers a shared, standardized way to report on livestock markets. Daily beef market reports using an early version of grading standards began in 1917. Those standards were refined and published in 1923, originally to help the U.S. Shipping Board and Veterans Bureau Hospitals buy beef consistently for large institutional orders — a bureaucratic origin story, not a chef's or a butcher's.

In 1925, the USDA held ten public hearings across the country, gathering input from producers, packers, purveyors, market reporters, and teachers on what the grades should measure. The revised standards were formally adopted in June 1926 as the Official United States Standards for Grades of Carcass Beef. In May 1927, the USDA rolled the program out as a free, voluntary service for a one-year trial. When the trial ended, the agency kept it running — but switched it to the voluntary, fee-based structure that, a century later, is still how it works today.

What a Grader Is Actually Looking At

Two factors drive a quality grade: marbling and maturity. Marbling is the flecking of intramuscular fat visible in a cross-section of muscle, typically assessed on the ribeye where the carcass is cut between the 12th and 13th ribs. Maturity is an estimate of the animal's age at slaughter, judged from the bone and cartilage structure of the carcass — younger animals show more cartilage and less bone ossification. More marbling and younger maturity combine to produce a higher grade. Marbling matters because that fat melts during cooking and bastes the muscle fibers from the inside, which is most of what people are actually responding to when they call a cut "tender" or "flavorful."

Why the Bar Kept Moving

The names Prime, Choice, and Select have stayed fixed since the 1920s. What qualifies for them hasn't. As American eating habits shifted toward leaner beef through the 1960s and '70s, the USDA reduced the marbling requirements for its top grades in 1965 and again in 1975, with further standards revisions in 1997. A steak that earns a Choice grade today does not carry the same marbling a Choice steak did in 1960 — the label held steady while the standard underneath it moved with the culture.

The label on the package hasn't changed since 1927. What qualifies for it has.

How to Actually Use the Grades at the Counter

If you're cooking something hot and fast — a weeknight steak, a quick sear — Prime justifies its price tag if you can find it and want to pay for it, but Choice is close enough for most home cooking that the difference is hard to detect once it's plated. Select works perfectly well if you're braising, stewing, or giving it real time in a marinade; slow, moist cooking compensates for the leanness that would make it tough over a hot grill. And ungraded or store-branded beef isn't automatically a downgrade — it just means nobody paid the USDA to put a letter on it. Ask whoever's behind the counter; they usually know exactly what they're selling even when the package doesn't spell it out.
