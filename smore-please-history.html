S’more Please

Three ingredients, sticky fingers, and a history that starts earlier than the famous 1927 recipe.

Words by Don't Be Hangry  ·  Deeper Dive

The cracker breaks. The chocolate slips. A strand of marshmallow attaches itself to your hand, and suddenly everyone has an opinion about how dark it should have been toasted. A s’more turns three ordinary ingredients into an occasion. Its history belongs to outdoor cooking, especially the Girl Scouts, but the familiar origin story needs a closer look.

The Short Version

  • A classic s’more sandwiches toasted marshmallow and chocolate between graham crackers.
  • The name is a contraction of “some more.”
  • A recipe appeared in The Girl Scout Leader in 1925.
  • The famous 1927 Tramping and Trailing with the Girl Scouts recipe came later.
  • Girl Scouts helped popularize the dessert; no single inventor is firmly established.

Before the famous handbook

The year 1927 appears so often in s’mores histories that it can sound like an invention date. It is better understood as a milestone in the printed record. Girl Scouts' own historical material identifies a recipe published in The Girl Scout Leader in 1925, two years before the handbook usually cited as the beginning.

A 2025 investigation by National Geographic journalist Dustin Renwick, republished by Girl Scouts San Diego, traces the dessert through earlier newspaper accounts and the work of British camping instructor Christian “Cookie” Moore. She taught outdoor skills to American Girl Scout leaders, and reports from 1924 training gatherings mention a new dessert with versions of the name “Somemore.” Her May 1925 magazine article included the familiar cracker, chocolate and toasted-marshmallow sandwich.

That makes Moore an important figure in the treat's spread. It does not establish the first person ever to combine the ingredients. Teaching, publishing and popularizing a dish leave evidence that an improvised first bite may never leave.

What the 1927 recipe actually says

Page 71 of Tramping and Trailing with the Girl Scouts contains a recipe headed “Some More.” Its instructions call for toasting marshmallows and putting them with chocolate between graham crackers. The recognizable structure is already there: crisp outside, warm and sticky inside.

The recipe belongs in a camping handbook, which helps explain the dessert's practicality. The crackers provide something to hold. The chocolate arrives ready to portion. Marshmallows can be toasted individually. There is no pastry crust to roll, no batter to measure, and no serving bowl everyone needs to wait for.

A handbook is also a way of carrying a small discovery beyond the people who first enjoyed it. A camp dessert can travel with a leader, a troop, or a book until it feels like something everyone has always known.

The inventor story that does not hold up

Many accounts credit a troop leader named Loretta Scott Crew. Renwick's investigation reports that Girl Scouts' historian could find no record of her, and the 1927 recipe itself does not name her. The responsible approach is to leave that attribution out rather than repeat it until it sounds established.

The documented Girl Scout connection is strong enough without an invented certainty about one creator. Food history often becomes clearer when we distinguish who made something famous from who made it first.

A family resemblance, not proof of descent

The ingredient combination had other early expressions. MoonPie dates its beginnings to 1917, before the Girl Scout recipes discussed here. Its chocolate-coated marshmallow sandwich offers an obvious family resemblance to a s’more.

Resemblance is not a documented chain of invention. The packaged snack and the freshly assembled campfire dessert are different experiences. One is ready when you open the wrapper; the other depends on heat, timing and the person holding the roasting stick.

Why “some more” was the right name

“S’more” compresses “some more” into a word that almost orders the next serving for you. It suits a dessert assembled one at a time. Finishing one does not necessarily end the evening; it may just mean returning to the crackers.

The appeal is partly texture. The graham cracker gives a dry crunch against the yielding marshmallow. Warmth softens the chocolate. Pressing the sandwich together combines those textures while making a clean, dignified bite fairly unlikely.

The tradition lives in the making

Some people rotate a marshmallow patiently until it turns evenly golden. Others prefer a darker exterior. That small disagreement gives everyone something to discuss while dessert happens in front of them.

A s’more can be made indoors, and its flavors can become a cake, cookie or pie. The classic still asks very little: crackers, chocolate, toasted marshmallow, and a moment to put them together. The equipment is modest. The pleasure is participatory.

That is our reading of its lasting appeal. The history explains how the recipe circulated; the ritual explains why another generation reaches for the roasting sticks. “S’more please” remains a perfectly reasonable response.
