Better Gravy From Your Turkey Drippings

Separate the roasting juices, build the sauce gradually and decide whether a fat separator deserves space in your kitchen.

When the turkey comes out, the roasting pan contains the beginning of another dish. The useful part is the savory liquid and browned residue. The floating fat is useful too, but you want to choose how much goes into the gravy rather than letting the pan decide.

A fat separator makes that separation more convenient. It does not create flavor, rescue burned drippings or replace the saucepan and whisk. Understand the process first, then decide whether the extra tool would earn its storage space.

Start with the juices you actually have

Transfer the bird to its resting place and carefully pour the hot drippings into a suitable heatproof container. Strain out large solids. Let the liquid settle so the fat rises. If the roasting pan has good browned residue, loosen it with a little stock; keep genuinely burned, bitter residue out of the sauce.

The amount and saltiness of the juices depend on how the bird was prepared. A seasoned or brined turkey can produce salty drippings. Taste as you build the sauce, and use unsalted stock when you need control. Adding salt at the beginning is much harder to undo.

Two ways to separate the fat

For an occasional batch, let the juices settle in a heatproof jug and carefully spoon off the floating fat. Reserve some for the gravy rather than automatically discarding it all. Work steadily around hot liquid.

A purpose-built separator is more convenient when you regularly make gravy or sauces from roasting juices. Build the gravy in a saucepan

Use a familiar, reliable gravy recipe for quantities. The basic sequence is straightforward: warm a measured amount of reserved fat, stir in flour and cook it into a smooth paste, then gradually whisk in the separated juices and enough stock for the amount of gravy you need.

Add liquid in stages, whisking each addition smooth. Bring the sauce to a simmer and cook until the flour is cooked through and the gravy coats a spoon. If it is too thick, add stock a little at a time. If it is thin, give it time to simmer and reduce before adding more thickener. Taste before adding final seasoning.

The separator is finished with its job before this step. The balance of fat, flour and liquid, plus patient whisking, determines the sauce. An expensive jug cannot fix a rushed pan.

Give the whole meal a workable sequence

Have the heatproof container, saucepan, whisk and serving jug ready before the bird comes out. Make the gravy while the turkey rests, rather than discovering halfway through that the whisk is buried under the washing-up.
