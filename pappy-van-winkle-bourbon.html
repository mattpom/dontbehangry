Pappy Van Winkle: The Bourbon, the Family and the Myth

It is excellent whiskey. It is also a case study in what happens when time, scarcity and storytelling become almost impossible to separate.

Words by Don't Be Hangry  ·  Deeper Dive

Pappy Van Winkle has become shorthand for unobtainable bourbon: the bottle behind the bar that can cost more by the ounce than many good whiskeys cost by the bottle. The legend is real, but the name is often used loosely. Not every Van Winkle bottle is technically Pappy, greater age does not automatically mean better whiskey, and inflated resale prices are not the prices established by the producer.

The Short Version

  • Julian P. “Pappy” Van Winkle Sr. began selling whiskey for W.L. Weller & Sons in 1893.
  • He opened the Stitzel-Weller Distillery in Shively, Kentucky, on Derby Day in 1935.
  • The Van Winkle family and Buffalo Trace entered a joint venture in 2002; Buffalo Trace says all production now takes place there under family guidelines.
  • Only the 15-, 20- and 23-year bourbons carry the Pappy Van Winkle's Family Reserve name.
  • The 10-year bourbon, 12-year bourbon and 13-year rye belong to the wider Van Winkle collection but are not labeled Pappy.

Who was Pappy?

Julian P. “Pappy” Van Winkle Sr. started as a traveling salesman for the Louisville liquor wholesaler W.L. Weller & Sons. After Prohibition, the Weller business and A. Ph. Stitzel Distillery were combined. In 1935, at age 61, Pappy opened the new Stitzel-Weller Distillery in Shively, outside Louisville.

The distillery became closely associated with wheated bourbon—a recipe in which wheat replaces rye as the secondary flavoring grain. The result is commonly described as softer and less rye-spicy, although barrel selection, proof and aging remain just as important as the grain recipe.

The family lost the distillery, not the name

After Pappy died in 1965, his son Julian Van Winkle Jr. led the business. Stitzel-Weller was sold in 1972 during a difficult period for American bourbon. Julian Van Winkle III joined the family business in 1977, acquired what remained of the Old Hoffman Distillery in Lawrenceburg and continued aging, processing and bottling Old Rip Van Winkle whiskey.

In the mid-1990s, older Old Rip releases were renamed under the Pappy Van Winkle brand. The 20-year Family Reserve was the first Pappy label. Its 1996 recognition by the Beverage Testing Institute became a turning point, giving the mature bourbon national attention well before today's collector frenzy.

What actually counts as Pappy?

The official Van Winkle lineup contains six regularly listed expressions: Old Rip Van Winkle 10 Year, Van Winkle Special Reserve 12 Year, Pappy Van Winkle's Family Reserve 15 Year, 20 Year and 23 Year, plus Van Winkle Family Reserve Rye 13 Year.

Only the 15-, 20- and 23-year bourbons are labeled Pappy Van Winkle's Family Reserve. Bars, auctions and casual drinkers often call the entire collection Pappy, but the labels make a clear distinction. That distinction matters when someone is describing—or pricing—the bottle in front of you.

Where is it made now?

The Van Winkles entered a joint venture with Buffalo Trace Distillery in 2002. The family's official history says Buffalo Trace began producing Van Winkle bourbon in May of that year using the family's wheated recipe. Buffalo Trace states that all production now takes place at its Frankfort distillery under strict family guidelines.

That does not mean every bottle released immediately after 2002 contained whiskey distilled at Buffalo Trace. Whiskey carrying a 20- or 23-year age statement necessarily reflects production decisions made decades before bottling. The source of older releases has therefore changed over time as legacy stocks were depleted and Buffalo Trace-distilled whiskey reached the required ages.

Why is it so hard to buy?

Age-stated whiskey cannot be manufactured on demand. A 23-year bourbon begins with a barrel filled more than two decades before its release, when no producer can know precisely what future demand will be. Evaporation reduces the amount remaining in a barrel, and long aging can make some barrels too oak-heavy to use.

The official 2026 suggested retail prices listed by the Van Winkle family run from $129.99 for Old Rip 10 Year to $449.99 for Pappy 23 Year. Those figures are far below many advertised resale prices. The gap reflects allocation, collector demand and markups after bottles leave the producer—not a producer claim that the whiskey is worth thousands of dollars.

Scarcity creates fraud. Counterfeit bottles and refilled empties are documented risks in the prestige-whiskey market. Buy bottles and pours only through licensed, reputable sellers. A high price does not authenticate a bottle.

Is older automatically better?

No. An age statement tells you the minimum age of the whiskey in the bottle; it is not a score. More time in new charred oak can add concentrated spice, tobacco, leather and dark-fruit notes. It can also create dryness and overpower the grain. Some drinkers prefer the balance and higher proof of the younger releases, while others want the pronounced oak character of the oldest bottles.

Rarity changes a tasting before the first sip. The useful question is not whether the mythology is deserved. It is whether the whiskey in the glass gives you more pleasure than another bourbon available at a rational price.

What Pappy really represents

Pappy Van Winkle sits at the intersection of family continuity, genuine whiskey craft and modern luxury economics. The reputation was not invented from nothing. The family preserved its wheated-bourbon identity through a long downturn, and the mature releases helped demonstrate that American whiskey could command serious critical attention.

But a good history can become a market force of its own. Today the bottle signifies access as much as flavor. The honest way to approach it is without cynicism or worship: know which Van Winkle whiskey you are being offered, understand that the secondary price is not the suggested retail price, and taste it as bourbon rather than as a financial instrument.
